# Faulty charging system

## Summary

On riding mowers, lawn tractors and zero-turns, the engine keeps the battery charged while it runs. Most mowers have an internal alternator under the flywheel, called a stator: the flywheel passing over it makes electricity. Larger mowers may have an external alternator, like a car. When the stator/alternator, the regulator or another electrical part fails, the battery isn't recharged. It is rarely the main reason a mower won't start, but it leaves a weak battery that can't start the mower next time, or lets the mower die while mowing. Testing the output is easy; finding which part failed is hard, so most Sources send it to a mechanic (pro).

## Before you start

- Make sure the battery is good and holds a charge, and that the cables and wiring are clean, good and tight. Only then suspect the charging system.
- The giveaway: you have to charge the battery every time you use the mower.

## How to confirm

Charging output test with a volt-ohm meter set to read 12 volts DC (see [Test the battery](../procedures/test-the-battery.md)):

1. Engine off: red lead on the positive (+) terminal, black lead on the negative (−). It should read 12.4 to 12.8 volts (some batteries read higher depending on charge and condition). Write the number down.
2. Set the parking brake, start the engine and run it at about 3/4 throttle. Read the battery again.
3. It should read 13.2 to 13.9 volts or higher. Higher than the engine-off reading means the alternator is charging.
4. If the reading doesn't change, the charging system has a problem: possibly a bad regulator or a bad stator/alternator. Another test, or a repair shop, is needed to tell which.

Some charging systems are 15 amp and others 20 amp, depending on the manufacturer and engine.

Several brand articles instead point to an ohmmeter test of the charging system in a linked article; the steps aren't in those Sources.

> **Safety:** set the parking brake before running the engine for the test, and keep hands clear of moving parts.

## Fix

Have a mechanic familiar with mower charging systems, or your dealer, find the failed part. There are many expensive electrical parts that could be at fault, and most parts stores and mower centers won't take back electrical parts, so guessing and "throwing parts" at it gets costly. Meanwhile [charge the battery](../procedures/charge-the-battery.md) to keep mowing, and replace the battery if tests show it is bad.
