## page: problems/battery-keeps-dying
- alias: "Troy-Bilt mower battery keeps dying"
- alias: "every time I go to mow the battery is dead"
- causes-named: loose-or-corroded-battery-cables, faulty-charging-system, parasitic-battery-drain, bad-battery
- fact: battery keeps dying when cables loose/corroded, terminals corroded, charging system failed, ignition key left on, or battery bad.
- safety: wear gear to protect skin and eyes; follow operator's manual.

## page: causes/loose-or-corroded-battery-cables
- confirm: follow the red (positive) cable from battery to starter solenoid, then on to the starter; all secure and good connection.
- symptom: corrosion = white or green substance; makes weak charge and battery keeps dying.
- safety: BEFORE cleaning, disconnect the negative cable first to break the circuit; if the wrench touches frame/metal on a live battery it can arc and throw sparks, potentially blowing up the battery. Always wear safety glasses and skin protection. Then remove the positive (red). Positive is last off and first on.
- fix: clean cables/connections with baking soda and water: 4 cups water with about six rounded tablespoons baking soda to make a paste; spread on corrosion (it foams); rinse off with water, preferably outdoors (messy); wire brush speeds it up; cola soda also works to remove corrosion.

## page: causes/faulty-charging-system
- confirm: test with a volt-ohm meter (after cables are good): 1) set meter to read 12 volts (DC); 2) with engine off, red lead on positive (+) terminal, black lead on negative (-); reading should be 12.4 to 12.8 V (some read higher depending on charge rate and condition); write it down; 3) set parking brake, start engine; 4) same lead placement; run at about 3/4 throttle; reading should be 13.2 to 13.9 V or higher; if higher than the engine-off reading the alternator is charging.
- symptom: if reading doesn't change from engine-off reading, charging system problem: bad regulator or bad stator/alternator.
- fact: some systems are 15 amp, others 20 amp, depending on manufacturer and engine specifications.
- pro: have a professional mechanic (Troy-Bilt servicing dealer or experienced local mechanic) find the root cause; guessing and throwing parts is costly.
- tip: if cables are not dirty you can run this test any time; may also identify a bad connection.

## page: procedures/test-the-battery
- step 1: meter on 12 V; engine off; red lead to + terminal, black to -.
- step 2: reading 12.4 to 12.8 V is normal; then see charging-system test in causes/faulty-charging-system (13.2 to 13.9 V or higher running at about 3/4 throttle).

## page: causes/parasitic-battery-drain
- fact: ignition key left in the on position: won't affect the battery much unless there is an oil light on the dash or a 12 V accessory cord plugged into an accessory port (Troy-Bilt with accessory port). A weak battery then fails to turn the engine over; charge the battery.

## page: causes/bad-battery
- symptom: after an hour or two on the charger the battery won't charge above 12 volts -> bad, replace.
- fix: 12 V lawn and garden batteries from Troy-Bilt dealers, automotive stores, home improvement stores. Bring the old battery; core fee between $15 and $25 otherwise.
- fact: new batteries are usually fully charged, but one that sat on the shelf long may need charging first; charge on a charger or jump-start.
- fact: most batteries last at least 3 years with proper care; changing more often than every 3 years suggests a connection or battery problem.
- tip: battery care: keep cables clean; cables tight and battery securely held down; don't let battery freeze; keep fully charged in cold weather. Most batteries fail from vibration (plates break up inside), so make sure it sits securely in the tray. A battery that isn't fully charged can freeze and be damaged.
- fact: battery size: at least 300 cold cranking amps (CCA) if the deck uses an electric clutch; without electric clutch 250 CCA can work but bigger is better; the battery must fit the holder.

## page: procedures/charge-the-battery
- tip: author likes a charger with a trickle charge setting for very weak batteries.
- step 1 (method one): disconnect cables (positive last off, first on). If caps removable, check water level; if plates are not covered, add distilled water (well water not good; city water slightly better).
- step 2: hook up charger: red to positive (+), black to negative (-).
- step 3: check voltage with meter: 11.8 V needs little charge time; 10.1 V needs a long time.
- step 4: "slow bake" for a very low battery: charger at 2 or 4 amps (some chargers have 6/4/2 amps) for about an hour; recheck that voltage is rising; if so, another hour.
- method two: if reading isn't too low and you need to mow: charger up to 6 amps for about half an hour to reach about 12 V; remove charger and start the mower; keep throttle at 3/4 to full so the battery charges while mowing; check reading after mowing.

## page: procedures/jump-start-the-battery
- safety: confirm both batteries are 12 volts first.
- step 1: pull car next to the mower and shut off the car engine.
- step 2: red positive jumper cable to the mower's positive terminal, then to the car battery's positive terminal.
- step 3: black negative cable to the car battery's negative terminal; connect the black mower-side cable to the mower frame.
- step 4: set mower brake and start the mower; let it run a few minutes. Don't start the car engine; the car battery provides enough power.
- step 5: remove cables in the opposite order of installation. The mower's alternator keeps the battery charged.
- tip: check battery with a volt meter to see how it is charging.
