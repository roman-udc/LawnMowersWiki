## page: problems/battery-keeps-dying
- alias: "lawn mower battery keeps dying"
- alias: "what keeps draining my lawn mower battery"
- causes-named: loose-or-corroded-battery-cables, faulty-charging-system, bad-battery, parasitic-battery-drain
- fact: Source summary: battery keeps dying from loose battery cables; dirty or loose wiring connections; a faulty charging system; a bad battery; or leaving the ignition key in the on position. Author works at a lawn mower repair facility.
- safety: always disconnect the negative (black) cable from the battery before making any repairs to the electrical system.
- safety: follow operator's manual safety instructions; consult a professional if lacking skills/knowledge/condition to do the repair safely.

## page: causes/loose-or-corroded-battery-cables
- symptom: battery keeps dying (problems/battery-keeps-dying).
- confirm: check the cables for looseness on the battery and elsewhere. Look at the battery first, then follow the red (positive) cable down from the battery to the solenoid and on to the starter.
- confirm: connections that are green or white with a lot of corrosion around them can be the problem.
- fix: remove the negative cable from the battery first to break the circuit, then the positive (red). Mnemonic: positive cable is the last one off and the first one on.
- fix: clean cables and connections with baking soda and water: mix 4 cups water with about six rounded tablespoons baking soda into a paste; spread on corrosion; it foams while cleaning; rinse with water (messy, rinse outdoors). A wire brush speeds it up. Cola soda also works to remove corrosion. Replace cables that need it.
- safety: if the circuit is not broken and the wrench touches the frame or other metal while removing the battery, it can arc and throw sparks that could potentially blow up the battery. Always wear safety glasses and gloves when working with the battery.
- tools: wrench, baking soda, wire brush, safety glasses, gloves.

## page: causes/faulty-charging-system
- symptom: battery keeps dying (problems/battery-keeps-dying); voltage does not rise with engine running.
- fact: few charging systems on the market. Larger mowers may have an external alternator like a car; most mowers have an internal alternator under the flywheel.
- confirm: needs a volt-ohm meter set for 12 volts.
- step 1: with engine off, touch leads (red to +, black to -) to the battery; reading should be 12.4 to 12.8 volts (some batteries read higher depending on charge rate and condition). Write the number down.
- step 2: set the parking brake and start the engine; run at about 3/4 throttle; read the battery again.
- step 3: reading should be 13.2 to 13.9 volts or higher. If higher than the engine-off reading, the alternator is charging.
- fact: if the reading does not change from the first, the charging system has an issue; cause may be a bad regulator or a bad stator/alternator. Another test or a repair shop is needed to identify which.
- fact: some systems are 15 amp, others 20 amp, depending on manufacturer and engine.
- tip: if cables are clean, this test can be done any time; it may also reveal a bad connection.
- pro: shop needed to determine which charging part failed (regulator vs stator/alternator).
- tools: volt-ohm meter.

## page: causes/bad-alternator
- fact: no charge rise at 3/4 throttle points to bad regulator or bad stator/alternator (see faulty-charging-system).

## page: causes/parasitic-battery-drain
- fact: leaving the ignition switch on when leaving the mower does not affect the battery much unless there is an oil light on the dash or a 12-volt accessory cord is left plugged into a port installed on the mower.

## page: causes/bad-battery
- confirm: if the battery will not charge above 12 volts after at least an hour or two on a charger, replace it.
- fix: remove battery, take to an automotive or lawn and garden store. Stores usually charge a core fee ($15 to $25) unless the old battery is returned. Most new batteries are fully charged but may need charging if long on shelf; can put on charger before installing or jump-start.
- fact: most batteries last at least 3 years with proper care. If changing more often than 3 years there may be a connection or battery problem.
- tip: keep cables clean and tight and battery securely held down. Most batteries fail because of vibration; make sure it does not bounce in the tray. Plates can break up inside.
- fact: sizing: at least 300 cold cranking amps (CCA) if the deck runs on an electric clutch; without an electric clutch 250 CCA can work, but bigger is better (more CCA = stronger battery).

## page: causes/weak-or-dead-battery
- fact: a dead battery after leaving the key on (with oil light / accessory drawing) can be recharged; see charge-the-battery.

## page: procedures/charge-the-battery
- tools: battery charger, volt meter, distilled water.
- step 1 (Method One): disconnect battery cables (positive is last off, first on). If the battery has removable caps, check water level; if plates are not covered add distilled water (well water is not good; city water is a little better; distilled is best).
- step 2: hook the charger to the battery: red to positive (+), black to negative (-), same as the volt meter.
- step 3: check battery voltage. At 11.8 volts it needs little time; at 10.1 volts it needs a long time; lower voltage = longer charge.
- step 4: author puts a very low battery on a "slow bake": chargers may have 6, 4 or 2 amp settings; charge a very low battery on 2 or 4 amps for about an hour, check that voltage is rising, and if so charge another hour.
- fact: Method Two: if the reading is not too low and you need to mow, charge on up to 6 amps for about half an hour to reach about 12 volts, remove the charger and start the mower; keep throttle at 3/4 to full so the battery charges while running. After mowing, check the reading.

## page: procedures/jump-start-the-battery
- fact: jump from a good 12-volt battery such as a car; confirm both batteries are 12 volts first.
- step 1: pull the car next to the mower and shut off the car engine.
- step 2: connect red positive cable to the mower's positive terminal, then to the car battery's positive terminal.
- step 3: connect black negative cable to the car battery's negative terminal; connect the other black (mower side) cable to the frame of the mower.
- step 4: set the mower brake and start the mower; do not start the car engine (car battery gives enough power). Let the mower run a few minutes.
- step 5: remove cables in the opposite order of installation. The mower's alternator keeps the battery charged. Check with a volt meter.

## page: procedures/test-the-battery
- fact: battery voltage with engine off should read 12.4 to 12.8 V; engine at 3/4 throttle 13.2 to 13.9 V or higher (see faulty-charging-system for steps).

## page: guides/battery-care-and-charging
- fact: batteries typically last 3+ years; vibration is the main killer; keep tight and clean; 300 CCA for electric clutch decks; core charge $15-$25; charging methods and voltages as above.
