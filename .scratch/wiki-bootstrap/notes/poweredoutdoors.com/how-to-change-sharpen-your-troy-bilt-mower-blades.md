## page: procedures/sharpen-and-replace-the-blades
- fact: Change blades at least once a year (author starts each season with a new set). Sharpen every 25 hours of use (about twice a year for the average homeowner); more often with dirt and stones.
- fact: New blades need no sharpening (sharpened at manufacture, painted over the edge).
- tools: ratchet, work gloves, safety glasses, torque wrench, cordless impact gun (optional)
- safety: Remove spark plug boots and ignition key; safety glasses and gloves.
- step 1: Remove blades with socket and ratchet; use an impact gun if stubborn; or place a block of wood on the bolt head and hit it with a hammer to loosen.
- step 2: Remove bolt, blade and any washers; reinstall in reverse order. Reused blades must be balanced first.
- fact: Sharpening tools: 10" flat metal file, drill-powered blade sharpener, or disc grinder; vice; blade balancer or nail on wall; rags; wire brush; safety glasses; gloves.
- step: Clean blade with rag (wire brush for buildup); secure in vice; do one side, flip, do other.
- step: File: push at an angle in one direction; no sawing; remove rough spots.
- step: Drill sharpener: flat guide on flat part of blade with cutting edge slotted into the bevel; move up and down the edge.
- step: Angle/bench grinder: perpendicular to the edge, run along the length to level nicks, keep moving to remove a little metal at a time.
- step: Balance: equal weight each side; shave metal off the heavy side. Nail-on-wall method: nail head about an inch from wall; centre hole of blade over nail; remove metal from the side that hangs lower until level. Unbalanced blades shake the mower and can damage spindle housing and engine; an inexpensive blade balancer prevents big repair expense.
- tip: Author has dealer sharpen (better job, convenient; drop off and pick up couple of days later; keeps a second sharpened set on hand). Reasons to use a pro: safer; sharpening often cheaper than new blade; pro can grind out large nicks and gouges; properly balanced blade reduces vibration and deck damage.
- tip: Lawn chemicals can corrode deck components and fuse blades to the deck; remove blades periodically.

## page: causes/dull-or-bent-blade
- symptom: Uneven or bad cut. Small chips and dings can be sharpened out; large chips and gouges mean replace.
- mistake: Never straighten a bent blade even if slight; straightening weakens the metal and a piece can break off.
- confirm: Off the deck: lay on a new blade; if it doesn't lie flat it's bent. On the deck: park on hard level surface, measure ground to blade tip, rotate 180 degrees, measure again; more than 1/8" difference means replace.
- safety: Cracked blade: replace; can break off and send metal flying, injuring people or damaging house siding.
- tip: Walk yard first: sticks, rocks, toys bend/gouge blades; significant impact causes vibration from spindle housing damage and unbalanced blades. Sand/debris dull blades faster (sparse lawn = more sand; check/sharpen/replace more often).

## page: causes/worn-blade-sail
- symptom: Sand and dirt sucked into the deck wear the sail; top of sail looks thinner at the ends than toward the centre.

## page: causes/bent-spindle-or-bad-spindle-bearing
- confirm: With gloves, grab both blade ends and rock up and down; feel for movement, listen for knocking; if present remove spindle housings and check damage.
- fix: Usually can replace the Troy-Bilt spindle bearing or the whole spindle assembly.
