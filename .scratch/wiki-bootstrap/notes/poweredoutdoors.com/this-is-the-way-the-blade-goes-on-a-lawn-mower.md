## page: causes/blade-installed-upside-down
- symptom: problems/uneven-cut, problems/mower-is-vibrating
- fact: Blade goes on with the sail (curved high side) facing up toward the deck; the sail draws grass upright as the blade passes.
- confirm: Blades are upside down if the sail or fin points down.
- symptom: Grass looks beaten up, roughed up, almost dirty; very poor cut.
- fact: Upside-down blades can damage spindles and cutter housings.
- tip: Look for stamped GRASS SIDE or BOTTOM on the blade; that side faces the ground. Look for bolt marks on the blade to see which way the bolt goes. "Sail up."
- tip: Take one blade off at a time, sharpen and replace it before removing the next so you always have an installed blade to reference.

## page: procedures/sharpen-and-replace-the-blades
- fact: Blades turn clockwise when looking down at the deck from the operator station; counter-clockwise when looking from underneath.
- tools: ratchet, work gloves, safety glasses, optional cordless impact gun
- step 1: Remove the spark plug boot so the engine cannot start. Do not skip.
- step 2: Remove blades with sockets and ratchet (may need effort; cordless impact gun works).
- step 3: Stuck bolt: put a block of wood on the bolt head and hit it with a hammer to shock it loose.
- fact: Source says "most lawn mower blades are reverse threaded, also known as right-hand threaded" and removal is counterclockwise. [Source internal inconsistency: reverse threaded normally means left-hand; the counterclockwise-removal statement matches a right-hand thread.]
- fact: Torque: most blades mount with a cap screw or two, needing 40 to 80 foot-pounds depending on bolt size; procedures vary by manufacturer, see owner's manual.
- brand: Sockets from 9/16 up to 13/16 on many mowers; some mowers such as Kubota take 22 or 24 mm.
- tip: Do not sharpen to a pointed edge; it wears faster.
- tools: sharpening: 10" flat metal file, drill-powered blade sharpener, or disc/angle/bench grinder; vise; blade balancer or nail on the wall; rags; wire brush; safety glasses; work gloves
- step 4: Wear safety glasses and gloves; clean the blade with rags, wire brush if needed; secure in a vise; sharpen one side then flip and sharpen the other.
- step 5: File: push the file at a 45-degree angle in one direction along the blade, no sawing motion, until rough spots and uneven edges are gone.
- step 6: Drill sharpener: flat guide along the flat of the blade, cutting edge in the bevel slot; move up and down the blade edge.
- step 7: Grinder: hold perpendicular to the blade edge; keep it moving along the length, removing a little metal at a time.
- step 8: Balance: place the center hole over a nail on the wall; sharpen the end that hangs lower until level; or buy a blade balancer.
- fact: Dull blades beat the lawn and make it look brown; bent or unbalanced blades damage cutter housing, seals and spindles and can cause shaking.
- pro: Hardware/outdoor power equipment stores offer sharpening; Source gives reasons: safer, often cheaper than a new blade, can grind out large nicks and gouges, blade properly balanced.

## page: guides/mower-parts-that-wear-out
- fact: Four blade types: high-lift (high sail; best precise cut and stripes; standard on most new mowers; grass should be kept at minimum 3" and thick, few weeds), low-lift (shorter sail; dusty dry conditions and sandy areas, short grass; less air movement and less dirt drawn into cutter housings that wears seals), mulch blade (swooping shape, no straight edge; may need extra baffling and a chute blocker; manufacturers offer an optional mulch kit; chops clippings finely), shredding/gator blade (jagged edge on the sail; smaller clippings and leaves; thatch layer keeps lawn cool, retains water, protects grass from foot and mower traffic).
