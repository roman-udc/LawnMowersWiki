## page: guides/zero-turn-safety
- fact: author's position: zero-turns are not good on hills (not enough weight on the front; drive systems can't keep one side driving correctly when weight shifts to one side); no manufacturer has solved it. Zero-turns reached the market in 1965 via Excel Hustler (per article).
- fact: performance can be improved on slopes not greater than 15 degrees with a wide turf tire, reduced tire pressure, avoiding wet conditions, and not overrunning the hydraulic system.
- safety: operate only on slopes of 15 degrees or less; steeper needs another method. Author reports stories/photos of zero-turns rolling down hills into creeks, streams, rivers and lakes.
- fact: drive tires: turf tires (also turf trac, super turf) with a squarer edge hold hills better; lowering pressure in some helps. Author runs about 10 to 12 psi for gradual hills (his practice, not a spec); wider and softer is better for climbing; too much air spins and slides downhill, even in reverse.
- fact: don't overrun hydraulics: don't climb with the levers all the way forward and pedal to the floor; it reduces pump life; instead bring hydro speed down while the engine makes horsepower so the pump builds pressure; keep throttle between 3/4 and full to keep hydraulic oil moving and airflow around engine on hot days.
- safety: don't operate on hills in wet conditions: rubber tires and wet grass give poor traction; going downhill you can't steer or stop because zero-turns steer and stop with the wheels; steeper hill = faster slide.
- fact: alternatives for steep hills: weed whip for small hills; commercial walk-behind for larger; still cautious (one slip can cause bad injury).
- fact: walk-behind engine: small 2-cycle push mowers are used by many cities/schools because of oil aspects; a four-stroke engine held on its side too long runs oil to one side and can starve the oil pump (damage, replacement); if a push mower smokes on hills try a diagonal cut. Larger horsepower doesn't matter much.
- fact: transmission: hydraulic drive probably best for hills (speed range, control); belt drive speeds are fixed by gears (e.g., 2nd gear 3.5 mph, 4th 5.1 mph), belts slip often and may need replacing a couple times a year; author recommends a hydraulic walk-behind if available (opinion).
